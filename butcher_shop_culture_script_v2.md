# 5-MINUTE PRESENTATION SCRIPT: ORGANIZATIONAL CULTURE IN BUTCHER SHOPS

---

## OPENING (30 seconds)

**[Slide: Title - "The Culture of the Chop: Organizational Culture in Butcher Shops"]"

"Good morning everyone. Today we're exploring organizational culture—but not in a tech giant or a multinational corporation. We're looking at something much more traditional, much more hands-on: the butcher shop.

You might think a small local butcher doesn't have 'organizational culture'—but you'd be wrong. Every workplace has a culture. It's just... some are bloodier than others."

---

## WHAT IS ORGANIZATIONAL CULTURE? (45 seconds)

**[Slide: Definition with meat cleaver imagery]"

"So what exactly is organizational culture? 

It's the personality of the organization—how we do things, what's accepted and what's not. It's the norms, traditions, and rituals that define a workplace.

In a butcher shop, this isn't written in an employee handbook. It's learned. It's passed down from the master butcher to the apprentice. It's in the way you hold your knife, the way you talk to customers, the way you start at 4 AM while the rest of the world is still asleep.

Culture gives the business a baseline to work under—and in butchery, that baseline is built on skill, tradition, and trust."

---

## HANDY'S CULTURAL MODEL APPLIED (1 minute)

**[Slide: Handy's 4 Cultures with butcher shop examples]"

"Let's apply Charles Handy's framework to understand what type of culture dominates in butcher shops—and it's more varied than you might think.

**Power Culture** is common in small, family-run butchers. It's semi-autocratic. The head butcher makes the decisions. But here's where it gets interesting—you also see this with megalomaniac managers who think they're 'saving butchery' from a dying trade. They make all the calls, convinced only they know what's best.

**Role Culture** appears in larger operations or chains, where people get set in their ways. 'This is how we've always done it.' But you also see this with owners who worry too much about margins—everything becomes about following the process to protect the bottom line.

**Task Culture** emerges in busy periods—Christmas, Easter—when it's 'whatever it takes.' But some shops have this energy all the time. You know the ones—where the staff have the right fit, there's singing and dancing while breaking down a carcass, and somehow the work gets done with genuine camaraderie.

And then there's the **Person-Centered** element. Traditional butchery is male-dominated, and you get that hardy 'get on with it' attitude. Individual skill matters—the master butcher's reputation IS the shop's reputation. But this can also attract the creepy older butcher who gives off bad vibes and makes younger staff uncomfortable.

The culture isn't uniform—it depends on who's running the show."

---

## THE COMPONENTS OF BUTCHER SHOP CULTURE (1 minute)

**[Slide: 6 Components with visual examples]"

"Let's break down the six components of culture we see in butcher shops:

**VALUES**: Quality meat, customer trust, craftsmanship. These aren't just words—they're why customers drive past the supermarket to come here.

**NORMS**: The unwritten rules. You don't touch another butcher's knife. You clean as you go. You never sell something you wouldn't serve your own family.

**ARTEFACTS**: The physical signs—the white coats, the sawdust on the floor, the meat hooks, the block that's been in the family for three generations.

**ROUTINES**: Opening prep, the lunch rush dance, end-of-day cleanup—these rituals bond the team.

**SYMBOLS & STORIES**: Every shop has tales. 'Remember when old Jimmy broke his saw on that frozen turkey?' These stories pass culture to new apprentices.

**CEREMONIES**: The Christmas order book opening. The apprentice getting their first proper knife. These moments matter."

---

## DEAL & KENNEDY'S FRAMEWORK (45 seconds)

**[Slide: Risk vs Feedback matrix]"

"Using Deal and Kennedy's model—where culture depends on risk level and feedback speed—where do butcher shops fit?

They typically fall into **Work Hard / Play Hard** culture. 

The risk is relatively LOW—unless you count the occasional finger! But the feedback is HIGH and immediate. A customer tells you straight away if the cut isn't right. You see the quality of your work instantly.

There's also elements of **Process** culture—following food safety regulations, HACCP, traceability. Step by step, no shortcuts.

But during busy periods? It can feel like **Tough Guy/Macho**—high stress, rapid turnover of product, everything happening at once. And that 'get on with it' attitude? That's the male-dominated environment showing through."

---

## SOCIALIZATION IN THE TRADE (30 seconds)

**[Slide: Three stages with apprenticeship imagery]"

"How does a new apprentice learn this culture?

**Pre-arrival**: They've watched their dad or grandad. They think they know what they're getting into.

**Encounter**: Reality hits. The cold, the early starts, the physical work. And sometimes—the bad vibes from that creepy older butcher who doesn't think they belong. Some don't make it past week one.

**Metamorphosis**: They adapt. They learn. They become part of the team—if the culture lets them in. And eventually—they pass it on to the next generation."

---

## CONCLUSION (30 seconds)

**[Slide: Summary points]"

"So what have we learned?

Organizational culture isn't just for corporate boardrooms. It's alive and well in your local butcher shop—but it's complex.

It can be the singing-and-dancing camaraderie of a well-fit team, or the toxic environment of a margin-obsessed owner. It can be the proud tradition of craftsmanship, or the megalomaniac convinced they're saving a dying trade.

Understanding this culture helps us see why some traditional trades thrive while others struggle to retain staff.

Because at the end of the day, culture isn't about policies and procedures. It's about people—and whether they actually want to show up to work.

Thank you."
